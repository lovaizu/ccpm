# Style

The reader is the producing role. When it finishes, it writes every document so the reading holds to these rules, on top of the essentials, and where a rule does not fit, it judges the rule by its why.

- Do not use bold.

    The reader who finds a few words marked skips the rest; the sentence carries its own weight.

- Do not use tables.

    The reader reads across cells for what a sentence would say.

- Write each list item as one sentence that makes sense on its own, with its explanation beneath after a blank line, indented.

    The reader gets the list from its items and reads an explanation only where they want the why.

- Use headings no deeper than h3.

    Deeper levels make the reader track where they are instead of what is said.

- Use parentheses only where leaving them out would mislead.

    A parenthesis breaks the sentence the reader is following.

- Use plain words, with no jargon, coined terms or common words stretched into figures of speech, and one word for each thing.

    The reader reads on without stopping, and never reads one word two ways.

- Refer to people by their role, not by name.

    The reader learns what someone does, and the text stays true when people change.

- Keep a code example to 10 lines or fewer.

    The reader takes in the point instead of reading the code.

- Leave history, process notes and how a value came about out of a deliverable.

    The reader wants how things stand now; the history is kept in git.

- Put a diagram first in its section, drawn top to bottom, with few boxes and each arrow labelled with what passes.

    The reader grasps the section at a glance before reading the prose.

- In a README, show use as a scenario with realistic console output, not a mechanical list.

    The reader pictures themselves using the product.
