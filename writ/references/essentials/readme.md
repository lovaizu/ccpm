# README

The reader is someone meeting the product for the first time. When they finish, they know the benefits the product gives them, decide whether to use it, and can start using it.

- Does the opening give the benefits, the states the reader gets that they would choose the product for over what they would use instead?

    A benefit is a state the user gets, such as being able to leave the work to it without watching. What the product provides toward a benefit is a feature, and features belong in the design doc: the user is aware only of how to use the product, not of its features. A sentence that says what the product does, such as "it calls you back only for the decisions that are yours", is a feature even when the user would notice it, and mixed into the opening it leaves the reader to work out what they get.

- Can the reader picture the experience of using the finished product?

- Can the reader see at a glance the flow from starting to use it to getting a result, and what they do and get at each stage?

    Each stage leads to a benefit in the opening, and each benefit is reached at some stage. A stage that leads to no benefit is not needed; a benefit that no stage reaches is missing the way to get it.

- Can the reader picture the example as their own work?

    Choose a situation that shows the product's strengths and is close to the reader's work, for example, moving a JavaScript app to TypeScript. Do not use other companies' product names, or examples that copy a well-known app.

- Can the reader read to the end without knowing the product's internals?

    Put the names of internal roles, and exceptions that do not matter to a first-time user, in the design doc. For why the product takes this shape, link to the design doc from the very sentence where the reader would ask "why".

- Can a newcomer get the product and start using it from this README alone, and learn its license?

    For prerequisites, write only what is needed and why. There are many ways to prepare, depending on the reader's environment. Writing just one reads as if the others could not be chosen, and those already prepared get a procedure they do not need.
