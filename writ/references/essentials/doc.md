# Every document

Can the reader grasp the whole by reading from top to bottom, and, when they finish, do what they need to do?

- Can the reader read every sentence as written for them?

    Who reads, what they decide and do when they finish, and whether they read straight through or skim for the parts they need are what every other part is judged against. Left unsaid, no sentence can be judged needed or not. Two readers who need different things each read sentences meant for the other, so a document for both serves neither.

- Can the reader get what they need from this document alone, without reading sentences they do not need?

    Take a part away: if the reader would still decide and do the same, it does not belong; if the reader must decide something no part supports, a part is missing. Parts that pass the reader by are often written for someone else, such as a reviewer, an approver or someone rereading, or they repeat what the title, a diagram or another sentence already says, or tell how the decisions came about when the reader wants how things stand now, or restate what a tool or another mechanism already enforces and drift from it in time. Looking only for gaps lets a document grow with every fix.

- Does the reader get content from the very first sentence?

    The first sentences get the reader's freshest attention. Spent on the document itself, such as its place among others, how to read it or what it contains, they give the reader nothing the title and body do not.

- Can the reader recognize a flaw in the content as a flaw?

    When what trips the reader lies in the content rather than the writing, such as a missing rule or claims that contradict each other, smooth sentences hide it, and the reader acts on it unaware.

- Does the line of reasoning hold together when you read only the headings from top to bottom?

    A reader who reads straight through follows the argument by its headings, and one who skims finds a part by them. Headings fitted to a fixed pattern rather than to the claim, or to the words the reader would look for, give neither.

- Can the reader grasp at a glance who takes part, what passes between them, and in what order?

    In prose the reader has to assemble actors, handoffs, order, branches and changes of state in their head. A diagram shows them at once when it draws its section's key point, names on each arrow what is passed, and draws documents and files as actors so the reader sees who writes and reads them. A diagram of something else, or with more boxes than one glance holds, is looked at and gives nothing.

- Can the reader understand by reading from top to bottom, without going back and forth?

    Each jump back or ahead costs the reader their place. They jump when the conclusion comes late, when a word is used before it is explained, when the abstract pattern comes before the example that would make it clear, or when a decision's reason, cost or rejected option sits away from the decision.

- Can the reader scan a list without reading the explanations?

    A reader scanning a list reads only the items. An item that makes sense only with its explanation sends them into every explanation.

- Can the reader understand every word without explanation, and read the same word as the same thing?

    A word the reader must look up stops them, and a word read two ways sends them the wrong way. Jargon, coined terms, a common word stretched into a figure of speech, one thing called by two names, a person's name where their role is meant, and a name the user never sees all do this.

- Can the reader tell how certain each statement is?

    The reader acts on a statement as far as they trust it. An unchecked claim stated flatly, a proposal that reads as a decision, a check whose reach is not given, or a comparison that gathers only the facts favoring one side leads them to act wrongly.

- Is the style suited to the reader, and does it read as if a person wrote it?

    A tone that shifts, or habits that give away AI writing, such as filler, restating, retreating into generalities or hedged phrasing, make the reader distrust the text and skim it.

- Can the reader stop where missing something would hurt, without bold, parentheses or tables pulling their eyes away?

    Every marked word competes for the eye, so when many are marked the one that matters is missed. A parenthesis breaks the sentence the reader is following, and a table used for anything but comparing things on the same points makes them read across cells for what a sentence would say.
