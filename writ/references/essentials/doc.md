# Every document

Can the reader grasp the whole by reading from top to bottom, and, when they finish, do what they need to do?

- Can the reader read every sentence as written for them?

    With who reads, what they decide and do when they finish, and whether they read straight through or skim settled, every part can be judged by whether this reader needs it, and the reader finds every sentence is for them. Two readers who need different things each read what is meant for the other.

- Can the reader get what they need from this document alone, without reading sentences they do not need?

    When every part is one the reader needs, they decide and do what they came for without wading through the rest. Take a part away: if the reader would still decide and do the same, it does not belong; if the reader must decide something no part supports, a part is missing. Parts that pass the reader by are often written for someone else, such as a reviewer, an approver or someone rereading, or they repeat what the title, a diagram or another sentence already says, or tell how the decisions came about when the reader wants how things stand now, or restate what a tool or another mechanism already enforces and drift from it in time. Looking only for gaps lets a document grow with every fix.

- Does the reader get content from the very first sentence?

    The first sentences get the reader's freshest attention, so the key point placed there is what they take in best. Spent on the document itself, such as its place among others, how to read it or what it contains, they give nothing the title and body do not.

- Can the reader recognize a flaw in the content as a flaw?

    A reader who sees a flaw in the content, such as a missing rule or claims that contradict each other, can raise it or work around it. Covered with smooth sentences, it is acted on unaware.

- Does the line of reasoning hold together when you read only the headings from top to bottom?

    Headings that carry each section's claim let a reader who reads straight through follow the argument, and headings in the words the reader would look for let one who skims find their part. Headings fitted to a fixed pattern give neither.

- Can the reader grasp at a glance who takes part, what passes between them, and in what order?

    A diagram that draws its section's key point shows actors, handoffs, order, branches and changes of state at once, which prose makes the reader assemble in their head. It does so when each arrow names what is passed and documents and files are drawn as actors, so the reader sees who writes and reads them. A diagram of something else, or with more boxes than one glance holds, gives nothing.

- Can the reader understand by reading from top to bottom, without going back and forth?

    Read in order, the reader keeps their place and builds understanding as they go: the conclusion and what they gain first, the example before the pattern it shows, each word explained where it first appears, and each decision's reason, cost and rejected options beside it. Each jump back or ahead costs them their place.

- Can the reader scan a list without reading the explanations?

    When each item makes sense on its own, a reader scanning the list gets its meaning from the items alone. An item that needs its explanation sends them into every explanation.

- Can the reader understand every word without explanation, and read the same word as the same thing?

    Plain words, each used with one meaning, let the reader read on without stopping. Jargon, coined terms, a common word stretched into a figure of speech, one thing called by two names, a person's name where their role is meant, and a name the user never sees each stop them or send them the wrong way.

- Can the reader tell how certain each statement is?

    A reader who can tell checked facts from assumptions, proposals and decisions, and how far each check reached, trusts each as far as it deserves and acts rightly. An unchecked claim stated flatly, or a comparison that gathers only the facts favoring one side, leads them astray.

- Is the style suited to the reader, and does it read as if a person wrote it?

    A tone suited to the reader and kept to the end lets them trust the text and read it closely. Habits that give away AI writing, such as filler, restating, retreating into generalities or hedged phrasing, make them distrust it and skim.

- Can the reader stop where missing something would hurt, without bold, parentheses or tables pulling their eyes away?

    With few marks, the reader's eye stops where missing something would hurt. When many words are marked, the one that matters is missed; a parenthesis breaks the sentence being followed; a table used for anything but comparing things on the same points makes them read across cells for what a sentence would say.
