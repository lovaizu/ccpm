# `readme`

## Goal

Someone who meets the product for the first time starts using it, from the README alone, only when it serves their work.

## Practices

### Q1

Telling a colleague about the product, does the reader retell the opening from memory, so that they can weigh the product and pass it on without reading it again?

**Thinking**

The opening says in a few short sentences what the product is, whom it's for, where it isn't yet fit for use, and what it doesn't do. Its first sentence names the kind of thing the product is and the one thing it gives the reader, in words the reader already uses and with no feature name or mechanism, so that it says in short what the product is. A reader weighs the product and passes it on by what they remember of the opening.

**Steps**

1. Read only the opening, put it away, and say it as to a colleague. Where a part goes missing or comes out otherwise, shorten it, or put what the product does in place of each vague or praising word.

### Q2

Reading the gain, does the reader tell what the product would let them do, so that they know whether it's for them?

**Thinking**

Name whom the product is for by the work they want done, and the gain as the end they reach with the product, from the objectives of the product vision. A reader can tell from that whether the product serves their own end.

**Steps**

1. Name the group and the work more closely where a reader outside that group couldn't tell that the product isn't for them.

### Q3

Before installing, does the reader see what the product doesn't do that they might expect of it, so that a reader who needs that turns away before they install?

**Thinking**

List only what a reader could reasonably expect of the product and it doesn't do. A reader who needs it then learns that before they install.

**Steps**

1. Gather what readers might expect from similar products and from what readers ask, and ask the user whether each is in or out.

### Q4

Before installing, does the reader see where the product isn't yet fit for use, so that they don't rest their work on it unaware?

**Thinking**

Where the product isn't yet fit for use, such as a release still in development or one no longer kept up, say so at the top. A reader who starts on it otherwise finds out only once their work rests on it.

### Q5

Starting with nothing set up, does the reader install the product by the README's steps alone and see that it works, so that they can start without help?

**Thinking**

The requirements and the install steps serve a reader who has nothing set up, each as a command to copy, and end with a sign that the product works. The author's own setup hides the gaps.

**Steps**

1. From a machine or sandbox where nothing the product needs is in place, follow the steps as written and do nothing they don't say. If a step fails or needs what the README doesn't say, write that in and start again.

### Q6

After the walkthrough, does the reader see that the product would give them, on work of their own, the gain the opening promised, so that they choose it on what it does and not on what it claims?

**Thinking**

Show one real run of the use most readers come for, on work like theirs, from what the reader gives to the result they keep, with the product's real output. The reader then watches the gain happen, not only promised.

**Steps**

1. Pick the use from what readers ask for most and from the objectives of the product vision. The trace: `{inputs}` read to its end.
2. Take each exchange from the record of a real run, in the form the reader meets it, cutting only what the reader doesn't need to see the result.
3. If the result doesn't show the gain the opening promised, pick another piece of work or another use.

### Q7

For each problem they feel in the work, does the reader picture how the product makes it go away, so that they see it solves their own problem?

**Thinking**

Each problem the reader feels in the present way, in their own words, gets a figure in which they see it left in that way and gone with the product, and a sentence or two on what the product does that makes it go away. A reader chooses the product once they can picture their own problem gone.

**Steps**

1. Take the problems from the Concept section of the product vision, one where the present way falls short for each idea, and from what readers complain of in that way. The trace: `{inputs}` read to its end.
2. For each, take from the design what the product does that makes it go away. Ask the user each one the design doesn't settle.
3. Look at each figure as a newcomer, and where they couldn't picture from it what its line says, draw it again.

### Q8

At each point where they would ask whether the product can really do that, does the reader picture how it does it, so that they believe it before they try it?

**Thinking**

Each point a newcomer would doubt gets a figure of its own that draws what its line says, and a sentence or two on how the product does it. A reader believes a product can do a thing once they can picture how. A figure of the whole that answers several doubts at once shows none of them at a glance.

**Steps**

1. Read the README as a newcomer, and mark each thing it says that the present way can't do. Ask of each answer what the newcomer would doubt next, until no doubt remains. The trace: `{deliverable}` read to its end.
2. For each point, take how the product does it from the design. Ask the user each one the design doesn't settle.
3. Look at each figure as a newcomer, and where they couldn't picture from it what its line says, draw it again.

### Q9

Needing more than the README gives, such as fuller use or help, does the reader find where to go next, so that the README need hold only what they need to decide and to start?

**Thinking**

The README holds what the reader needs to decide and to start. For each later need, such as fuller use or help, it links one place. No one reads a README that holds everything, and each later need has its own kind of page, as [Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr) sorts tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation.

**Steps**

1. Open each link.

### Q10

Weighing whether they may use the product, does the reader find the terms for them and their team, so that they can rule it in or out on its license?

**Thinking**

Name the license and link its full text. A reader may rule the product out by its license alone.

**Steps**

1. Open the link, and check that it reaches the license the name says.

### Q11

Reading from the top, does the reader find each answer just when they come to need it, so that they can stop once they have what they came for?

**Thinking**

Order the README so that each reader stops once they have what they came for: the opening for every reader, the installation for one who has chosen the product, the walkthrough and How it works for one still weighing it, and what comes after through links. A reader who must pass what they don't need leaves before what they do, as the [inverted pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/) puts first what every reader needs.

**Steps**

1. Read the README from the top as someone who meets the product for the first time, and after each section, write the question the reader now has. The trace: `{deliverable}` read to its end.
2. Check that the next part answers that question. Move a section that answers a later one.
3. Cut what no reader needs at the point where it stands, or move it to the document a link leads to.
