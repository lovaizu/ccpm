# README

A README is read by someone meeting the product for the first time, with only what a newcomer has.
From it alone they decide whether the product is for them, and if it is, they get to their first
result with it. Someone joining those who build the product finds the way to its architecture and
development documents from it.

A README tells a newcomer what the product holds for them before anything they must do, so its
order follows their decision. The opening gives the idea the product rests on, whom it is for and
what they gain, from the users, goals and benefits of the product's UX. The example, one of the UX's
scenes, shows using the product before the reader decides to install it. Why sets the product
against other ways of doing the same work, right after the use the reader has just seen. What
follows takes a reader who has decided to their first result. What lies beyond the first result,
such as fuller use, goes in a page that Documentation names. The headings are those READMEs commonly
give, so the reader finds requirements, installation or the license where they look for them.

## Format

```markdown
# <Product name>

<The idea the product rests on, in one sentence; then what the reader gains, with the kind of thing
that gives it, and whom it is for, in one or two sentences; under no heading>

## Example

<Using it on one piece of work, from starting to the result, with what the product gives back>

## Why <product name>

<What only this product gives, set against the other ways of doing the same work>

## Requirements

- <Each thing that must be in place beforehand, said as what it is>

  <Why it is needed, where the reader would not know>

## Installation

<How to install, then the first use, as in the example>

## Documentation

<Where fuller use and what changed in each version are written>

## Development

<A link to the entrance to the architecture and development documents>

## License
```

## Questions

### Q-1: Whether the product is for them

From the opening, can the reader tell whether the product is for them?

Why: the reader decides from the opening whether to read on. One shown only what the product does
takes it for any tool that does the same, so the opening gives the idea that sets it apart. One who
cannot place themselves among those it is for leaves a product that would have served them.

### Q-2: Using it on their own work

From the example, can the reader picture using the product on their own work, from starting to the
result?

Why: a reader who can picture it decides on what they will actually get. An example on work of
another kind, or one without what the product gives back as the reader gets it, is read as someone
else's story.

### Q-3: Why this one

From Why, can the reader tell what the product gives them that doing the work by hand, or with
another tool, does not?

Why: what only this product gives is the reason to choose it. What every way of doing the work
gives, such as not losing work, does not decide the choice, and placed first it hides what does.

### Q-4: The first result from the README alone

Can a newcomer, with only what a newcomer has, get ready what must be in place, install the product
and get the example's result, by following the README alone?

Why: each place where the reader must look elsewhere, or where a step fails, is a place they may
stop. Whoever wrote the README has the product at hand and never meets it. A requirement said as
what it is lets a reader who already has it, or would get it another way, tell so.

### Q-5: Each way on reached

Can the reader reach each place that Documentation, Development and License send them to?

Why: a README stays short only when the rest is somewhere the reader can reach. One who cannot find
the architecture changes the product without the decisions behind it, and one who cannot tell the
terms of use cannot decide to use it.

### Q-6: Each promise kept by the product

Does the product, when the reader runs it, do what each promise of the opening and Why says, as
widely as it says?

Why: a promise wider than what the product does is found out by the reader at their first use, and
a README that has turned out wrong is worse than none, as it was believed.
