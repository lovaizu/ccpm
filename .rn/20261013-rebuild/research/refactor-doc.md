# Research: refactor-doc

Sources for the best practice of refactor-doc: how a document says what it says, so that its reader understands it as meant on one reading.
The reader may read it in its own language or through a machine translation into theirs.
The user is that reader, who comes to the document for a purpose.
Every quote below was copied from the page at its URL, opened on 2026-11-02.
The plainlanguage.gov guideline pages are cited from the GSA archive on GitHub, because the site itself now points there.

## The reader comes to the document for a purpose

### Readers find, understand and use

- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/media/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf
- Quote:

  > We hope you find this document useful, and that it helps you improve your writing — and your agency’s writing — so your users can:

- What it says: the three aims that follow are "find what they need," "understand what they find;" and "use what they find to meet their needs."

### Write for the audience you actually have

- Source: PLAIN, "Write for your audience", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/audience/index.md
- Quote:

  > Know the expertise and interest of your average reader, and write to that person. Don't write to the experts, the lawyers, or your management, unless they are your intended audience.

- What it says: the document is written for the reader who will use it, not for the reviewers around the writer.

### Ask what the reader knows and needs

- Source: PLAIN, "Write for your audience", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/audience/index.md
- Quote:

  > Think about what your audience knows about the situation or topic you're writing about. Then, guide them through the information they need to know.

- What it says: the page lists questions such as "What does my audience already know about the subject?" and "What questions will my audience have?"

### Readers come with a task

- Source: PLAIN, "Do your research", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/audience/do-your-research.md
- Quote:

  > Understanding what users are trying to do when visiting your website or reading your communications will help you write clearly and focus on their needs.

- What it says: the reader's top tasks decide what the document holds and where.

### Documentation is organised around the reader's needs

- Source: Daniele Procida, "Diátaxis", diataxis.fr, undated web page.
- URL: https://diataxis.fr/
- Quote:

  > Diátaxis identifies four distinct needs, and four corresponding forms of documentation - tutorials, how-to guides, technical reference and explanation. It places them in a systematic relationship, and proposes that documentation should itself be organised around the structures of those needs.

- What it says: a document's form follows from which need its reader brings.

### Most readers scan

- Source: Jakob Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web", Nielsen Norman Group, 1997-09-30.
- URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/
- Quote:

  > In research on how people read websites we found that 79 percent of our test users always scanned any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word.

- What it says: a reader reads only the parts their purpose needs, so the document must let them find those parts.

### Assume the reader scans

- Source: Kara Pernice, "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant (Even on Mobile)", Nielsen Norman Group, 2017-11-12, last reviewed 2026-08-19.
- URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/
- Quote:

  > If people are highly motivated and interested in content, they will read all the text in a paragraph or even an entire page. (Don’t count on this to happen frequently, though. Assume that most users will be scanning.)

- What it says: reading every word is the exception, so structure must carry the reader to what they need.

## The reader finds the parts they need

### State the purpose and the bottom line first

- Source: PLAIN, "Organize the information", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/index.md
- Quote:

  > Organization is key. Start by stating your purpose and the bottom line. Lay things out in a logical order. Put the most important information at the beginning and include background information (when necessary) toward the end.

- What it says: the opening tells the reader what the document is for and what it concludes.

### Each paragraph opens with its topic

- Source: PLAIN, "Have a topic sentence", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/have-a-topic-sentence.md
- Quote:

  > Readers should be able to get good general understanding of your document by skimming your topic sentences.

- What it says: the first sentence of each paragraph carries its point, so a skimming reader still gets it.

### Do not make the reader hold details before the point

- Source: PLAIN, "Have a topic sentence", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/have-a-topic-sentence.md
- Quote:

  > We often write the way we think, putting our premises first and then our conclusion. It may be the natural way to develop thoughts, but we wind up with the topic sentence at the end of the paragraph. Move it up front and let users know where you're going. Don't make readers hold a lot of information in their heads before getting to the point.

- What it says: the order of thinking is not the order of reading.

### Headings show the organisation

- Source: PLAIN, "Add useful headings", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/add-useful-headings.md
- Quote:

  > Well organized writing will still be difficult for users to follow if they can't see how it's organized. An effective way to reveal your document's organization is to use lots of useful headings.

- What it says: the page ranks question headings first, statement headings next, and topic headings last, because topic headings can be "so vague that they just aren't helpful."

### A section that is too long cannot get a useful heading

- Source: PLAIN, "Write short sections", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/concise/write-short-sections.md
- Quote:

  > Long sections are impossible to summarize meaningfully in a heading. When you break up different concepts into short sections, each heading can give the reader a clear picture of what's in that section.

- What it says: one concept per section is what lets its heading tell the reader what is there.

### Lead headings and paragraphs with the words that carry the information

- Source: Kara Pernice, "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant (Even on Mobile)", Nielsen Norman Group, 2017-11-12, last reviewed 2026-08-19.
- URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/
- Quote:

  > Start headings and subheadings with the words carrying most information: if users see only the first 2 words, they should still get the gist of the following section.

- What it says: the same page advises "Include the most important points in the first two paragraphs on the page."

### Lead with what matters most to the reader

- Source: Microsoft, "Scannable content", Microsoft Writing Style Guide, 2023-06-20.
- URL: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/
- Quote:

  > Lead with what's most important. Place important keywords near the beginning of headings, table entries, and paragraphs so they're easy to spot.

- What it says: the start of every unit is where the reader looks first.

### Title a document by its primary purpose

- Source: Google, "Headings and titles", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2026-06-08.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/headings
- Quote:

  > Write document titles based on the primary purpose of the document. If a document is primarily a tutorial, but it has a conceptual introduction, write a task-based title.

- What it says: the title tells the reader which need the document serves.

### Put steps in the order the reader follows them

- Source: PLAIN, "Make it easy to follow", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/make-it-easy-to-follow.md
- Quote:

  > Present the steps chronologically, in the order your user and your agency will follow them.

- What it says: a process is told in the reader's order of doing it.

## The reader sees order and relations at a glance

### Structure itself tells the reader how to interpret

- Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist, 1990, reprinted in Schall, "Effective Technical Writing in the Information Age", Humanities LibreTexts.
- URL: https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Technical_Composition/Effective_Technical_Writing_in_the_Information_Age_(Schall)/10%3A_Journal_Articles_about_Writing/10.06%3A_The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing
- Quote:

  > Readers do not simply read; they interpret. Any piece of prose, no matter how short, may "mean" in 10 (or more) different ways to 10 different readers. This methodology of reader expectations is founded on the recognition that readers make many of their most important interpretive decisions about the substance of prose based on clues they receive from its structure.

- What it says: the article gives the same readings as an unordered line and as a table, and says "most readers find the second easier to interpret."

## The reader takes in each sentence on first reading

### Keep subject, verb and object close

- Source: PLAIN, "Keep the subject, verb, and object close together", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/concise/keep-the-subject-verb-and-object-close-together.md
- Quote:

  > The natural word order of an English sentence is subject-verb-object. This is how you first learned to write sentences, and it's still the best way. When you put modifiers, phrases, or clauses between two or all three of these essential parts, you make it harder for the user to understand you.

- What it says: anything placed between the subject and its verb makes the reader hold the subject in mind.

### Follow the subject with its verb, and put the action in the verb

- Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist, 1990, reprinted in Humanities LibreTexts.
- URL: https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Technical_Composition/Effective_Technical_Writing_in_the_Information_Age_(Schall)/10%3A_Journal_Articles_about_Writing/10.06%3A_The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing
- Quote:

  > Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.

- What it says: the article's list of principles also includes "Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb."

### Old information first, new information last

- Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist, 1990, reprinted in Humanities LibreTexts.
- URL: https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Technical_Composition/Effective_Technical_Writing_in_the_Information_Age_(Schall)/10%3A_Journal_Articles_about_Writing/10.06%3A_The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing
- Quote:

  > Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize.

- What it says: the reader links each sentence to the one before by its start, and takes its point from its end.

### Give context before anything new

- Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist, 1990, reprinted in Humanities LibreTexts.
- URL: https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Technical_Composition/Effective_Technical_Writing_in_the_Information_Age_(Schall)/10%3A_Journal_Articles_about_Writing/10.06%3A_The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing
- Quote:

  > In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new.

- What it says: the authors add "There can be no fixed algorithm for good writing," because "any reader expectation can be violated to good effect."

### No sentence can be made to mean only one thing

- Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", American Scientist, 1990, reprinted in Humanities LibreTexts.
- URL: https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Technical_Composition/Effective_Technical_Writing_in_the_Information_Age_(Schall)/10%3A_Journal_Articles_about_Writing/10.06%3A_The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing
- Quote:

  > We cannot succeed in making even a single sentence mean one and only one thing; we can only increase the odds that a large majority of readers will tend to interpret our discourse according to our intentions.

- What it says: whether readers take a sentence as meant is a matter of odds, so it is checked with readers, not only by rules.

### One idea in each sentence

- Source: PLAIN, "Write short sentences", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/concise/write-short-sentences.md
- Quote:

  > Resist the temptation to put everything in one sentence; break up your idea into its parts and make each one the subject of its own sentence.

- What it says: the page opens with "Express only one idea in each sentence."

### Active voice shows who does what

- Source: Google, "Active voice", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2024-10-15.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/voice
- Quote:

  > In passive voice, it's easy to neglect to indicate who or what is performing a particular action. In this kind of construction, it's often hard for readers to figure out who's supposed to do something (such as the reader, the computer, the server, an end user, or a visitor to a web page).

- What it says: a sentence that names its actor tells the reader whether they are the one to act.

### Use the verb, not a noun made from it

- Source: PLAIN, "Avoid hidden verbs", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/avoid-hidden-verbs.md
- Quote:

  > A hidden verb (or nominalization) is a verb converted into a noun. It often needs an extra verb to make sense. For example, "Please make an application for a personal loan" is longer and less clear than "Please apply for a personal loan."

- What it says: the page adds that hidden verbs "often go hand in hand with passive verbs".

### Break up strings of nouns

- Source: PLAIN, "Avoid noun strings", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/avoid-noun-strings.md
- Quote:

  > Technically, clustering nouns turns all but the last noun into adjectives. However, many users will think they've found the noun when they're still reading adjectives, and will become confused.

- What it says: the fix is "using more prepositions and articles to clarify the relationships among the words."

### No more than two nouns as modifiers

- Source: Google, "Write for a global audience", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2026-10-02.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/translation
- Quote:

  > Don't use too many modifiers. In particular, don't use more than two nouns as modifiers of another noun.

- What it says: the same page says to place "only" "immediately before the word or phrase that it relates to."

### Do not chain many clauses

- Source: Microsoft, "Global communications: Writing tips", Microsoft Writing Style Guide, 2023-06-14.
- URL: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/global-communications/writing-tips
- Quote:

  > Avoid linking more than three phrases or clauses by using coordinate conjunctions such as and, or, or but. Better yet, avoid linking more than two.

- What it says: a long chain of "and" clauses hides where one point ends and the next begins.

### Use standard word order

- Source: Microsoft, "Global communications: Writing tips", Microsoft Writing Style Guide, 2023-06-14.
- URL: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/global-communications/writing-tips
- Quote:

  > Write sentences that use standard word order (that is, subject + verb + object) whenever possible.

- What it says: the tip stands in the page's list for machine translation.

### Replace an ambiguous pronoun with its noun

- Source: Google, "Write for a global audience", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2026-10-02.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/translation
- Quote:

  > For example, if a pronoun is ambiguous, then replace it with the appropriate noun.

- What it says: the tip is headed "Clarify antecedents.", because translators often work with "small, unconnected strings of text."

## Each word means one thing to every reader

### One term for one concept, also for translation

- Source: Google, "Write for a global audience", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2026-10-02.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/translation
- Quote:

  > If you use a particular term for a concept in one place, then use that exact same term elsewhere, including the same capitalization. If you use different names for the same thing, translators might think you're referring to different concepts, and thus might use different translations.

- What it says: a synonym makes a reader, or a translator, think a second thing is meant.

### One word for a concept, and one concept for a word

- Source: Microsoft, "Global communications: Writing tips", Microsoft Writing Style Guide, 2023-06-14.
- URL: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/global-communications/writing-tips
- Quote:

  > Use one word for a concept, and use it consistently. Avoid using synonyms to refer to the same concept or feature. And don’t use the same word to refer to multiple concepts or features.

- What it says: the rule runs both ways, against synonyms and against one word with two meanings.

### Readers with limited English are confused by synonyms and many meanings

- Source: ASD Simplified Technical English Maintenance Group (STEMG), "About ASD-STE100", asd-ste100.org, page copyright 2023; the specification's current issue is Issue 8, April 2021.
- URL: https://www.asd-ste100.org/about.html
- Quote:

  > Many readers have limited knowledge of English, and are easily confused by complex sentence structures and by the number of meanings and synonyms which English words can have.

- What it says: STE's answer is "one word – one meaning", for example "start" chosen over "begin", "commence", "initiate", or "originate".

### One word for one meaning

- Source: ASD STEMG, "About ASD-STE100", asd-ste100.org, page copyright 2023.
- URL: https://www.asd-ste100.org/about.html
- Quote:

  > In general, there is only one word for one meaning, and one part of speech for one word.

- What it says: the specification's dictionary keeps one word for each meaning; the old best practice quoted "each word has only one meaning and is approved with only one part of speech", which this page does not say.

### Do not vary terms to make writing interesting

- Source: PLAIN, "Use the same terms consistently", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/use-the-same-terms-consistently.md
- Quote:

  > Using a different term may cause the reader to wonder if you're referring to the same group.

- What it says: the page adds "You don't need to use synonyms to make your writing more interesting."

### Writers miss that their terms are new to the reader

- Source: PLAIN, "Avoid jargon", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/avoid-jargon.md
- Quote:

  > writers often fail to realize that terms they know well may be difficult or meaningless to their audience.

- What it says: a word the writer's team uses every day is still new to the reader.

### Readers forget a common word's new meaning

- Source: PLAIN, "Minimize definitions", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/minimize-definitions.md
- Quote:

  > readers are likely to forget that you'd assigned some common word a new meaning, and when they come upon the word later in your document they assign the common meaning, rather than your specialized one.

- What it says: a common word given a meaning of the document's own is read with its common meaning.

## Each fact is said in one place

### Shorter sentences translate better

- Source: Google, "Write for a global audience", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2026-10-02.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/translation
- Quote:

  > Write shorter sentences. The shorter the sentence, the easier it is to translate. English sentences can be shorter in length than some languages, so an English sentence of average length might result in a long sentence when translated.

- What it says: a sentence of average length in English can become long in the reader's language.

### Rules that helped older machine translation may not help neural translation

- Source: Shaimaa Marzouk, "An in-depth analysis of the individual impact of controlled language rules on machine translation output: a mixed-methods approach", Machine Translation 35:167–203, 2021, doi:10.1007/s10590-021-09266-0, open-access copy in the Mainz university repository.
- URL: https://openscience.ub.uni-mainz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/26bfc90c-dfcc-4368-8c15-a343585a6c07/content
- Quote:

  > Rules that had a positive impact on earlier MT approaches did not show the same impact on the neural MT approach. Furthermore, neural MT delivered distinctly better results than earlier MT approaches, namely the highest error-free, style and content quality rates both before and after applying the rules, which indicates that neural MT offers a promising solution that no longer requires CL rules for improving the MT output.

- What it says: the study translated German technical text into English, so a rule for translation needs evidence for the systems in use now.

### Some rules helped and some hurt

- Source: Shaimaa Marzouk, "An in-depth analysis of the individual impact of controlled language rules on machine translation output: a mixed-methods approach", Machine Translation 35:167–203, 2021.
- URL: https://openscience.ub.uni-mainz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/26bfc90c-dfcc-4368-8c15-a343585a6c07/content
- Quote:

  > However, at rule level, only four rules proved to have positive effects on the aforementioned parameters; three rules had negative effects on the parameters; and two rules did not show any significant impact.

- What it says: the rules that helped included avoiding light-verb constructions and writing conditions as "if" sentences; "pas—Avoiding passives" hurt.

## Conditions and exceptions sit where the reader can use them

### Put the condition before the instruction

- Source: Google, "Sentence structure", Google developer documentation style guide, last updated 2024-10-15.
- URL: https://developers.google.com/style/sentence-structure
- Quote:

  > If you want to tell the reader to do something, try to mention the circumstance, conditions, or goal before you provide the instruction. Mentioning the circumstance first lets the reader skip the instruction if it doesn't apply.

- What it says: a reader whose case does not apply can stop reading at the condition.

### Put long conditions after the main clause

- Source: PLAIN, "Place words carefully", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/words/place-words-carefully.md
- Quote:

  > Put long conditions after the main clause. Write "complete form 9-123 if you own more than 50 acres and cultivate grapes," not "if you own more than 50 acres and cultivate grapes, complete form 9-123."

- What it says: this contradicts the Google rule above for long conditions; the sources agree only that the main point must not be buried.

### Main idea before exceptions, with no absolute rule

- Source: PLAIN, "Place the main idea before exceptions and conditions", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/place-the-main-idea-before-exceptions-and-conditions.md
- Quote:

  > There is no absolute rule about where to put exceptions and conditions. Put them where they can be absorbed most easily. In general, the main point of the sentence should be as close to the beginning as possible.

- What it says: the placement is judged by what the reader can take in, which settles the conflict above case by case.

## How the document is checked: test it with readers

### Test early, revise and test again

- Source: PLAIN, "Test your assumptions", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/test/index.md
- Quote:

  > Test as early as you can in the project, whether you're creating something new or making revisions. Test, make corrections based on feedback, and test again. Plan to test at least twice.

- What it says: the guideline treats testing as part of writing, not a step after it.

### Paraphrase testing shows what the reader thinks it means

- Source: PLAIN, "Paraphrase testing", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/test/paraphrase-testing.md
- Quote:

  > Paraphrase testing will tell you what a reader thinks a piece of writing means and will help you know if they're interpreting the message as you intended.

- What it says: the reader says each section back in their own words, and every mismatch marks a place to fix.

### Do not correct the reader; fix the document

- Source: PLAIN, "Paraphrase testing", GSA plainlanguage.gov archive on GitHub, undated page.
- URL: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/main/_pages/guidelines/test/paraphrase-testing.md
- Quote:

  > Do not correct the participant. When you review your notes later, wherever participants misunderstood the message, the document has a problem that you should fix.

- What it says: a misunderstanding is a fault of the text; the page also says to "Avoid yes or no questions".

### Concise and scannable text measured better

- Source: Jakob Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web", Nielsen Norman Group, 1997-09-30.
- URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/
- Quote:

  > As shown in the table, measured usability was dramatically higher for the concise version (58% better) and for the scannable version (47% better).

- What it says: the same content, rewritten to be concise and scannable, let users do their tasks better.
